Lesson 7

Collisions!

Things finally touch each other — and that is when it becomes a game.

TimingOne period (~50 min)
New ideaDetecting when two sprites overlap, and removing things from a list
Builds onLesson 6 — if, score, and gameOver.
StandardUse collidesWith() with if to trigger game events, remove from a list safely, and connect collisions to score and state.

Warm-up (~5 min)

What does an if statement do? What is a boolean? Name one from your own game.

Direct instruction (~10 min)

collidesWith() — and why they already have it

"The library checks whether the two circles overlap and returns true or false."

Make the connection explicit: because MyCharacter extends Blob, their character can use collidesWith() against any other Blob. This is the payoff for the one-word decision back in Lesson 2, and it is worth naming so extends feels like it earned its keep.

Do not say the word *inheritance*.

The hitbox is the radius from super()

Circle back to the 30 in their Lesson 2 constructor. That is the collision circle, and it has nothing to do with how big their art looks.

A big radius is forgiving; a small one is precise. Students whose character art drifted far from their radius will get collisions that feel wrong, and this is the fix.

Show the bug before the fix

Run a forward loop with a remove() inside it on the projector. Collect coins and let students watch items get skipped — some coins become uncollectable.

Seeing the skipping artifact makes the backwards loop feel like a solution instead of an arbitrary incantation. This is the highest-value ninety seconds of the lesson.

Then explain the backwards loop

"If you walk forward through a list and remove something, everything after it shifts back one, and you skip the item right after it. Walking backwards avoids that problem."

Students do not need to memorize this or derive it. Teach it as "the safe loop for removing things" — a pattern they can recognize and copy.

The pattern to copy
for (int i = coins.size() - 1; i >= 0; i--) {
  Blob c = coins.get(i);
  if (player.collidesWith(c)) {
    coins.remove(i);
    score = score + 1;
  }
}

Wire it to Lesson 6

The collision changes score. The score/size() check flips gameOver. The gameOver boolean drives the if/else in draw().

Trace that chain out loud once. It is the first time the whole machine connects end to end, and students who see it will build Lesson 9 much more confidently.

Guided activity (~20 min)

  1. Everyone opens Lesson 6's sketch and finds the for-each coin loop inside playGame().
  2. Replace it with the backwards index loop, with no collision check yet. Run. Nothing looks different — that is the point, the loop is equivalent until you remove something.
  3. Add the collidesWith() check with remove() and the score increment. Run. Everyone collects a coin. Let the room react.
  4. Show the forward-loop bug on the projector if you did not already.
  5. Switch the win condition to coins.size() == 0 and let someone clear the board.
  6. Anyone whose collisions feel wrong: open MyCharacter and tune the radius in super(...).
  7. Introduce hazards as the main personalization task. Second ArrayList, for-each loop (no removal), gameOver = true on contact. This directly sets up Lesson 9.

Common mistakes to watch for

  • ConcurrentModificationException — removing inside a for-each loop. The crash message is intimidating and means nothing to a sixth grader. Translate it: "you changed the list while walking through it." The backwards loop is the fix.
  • Off-by-one: writing coins.size() instead of coins.size() - 1, which throws an index-out-of-bounds on the first pass.
  • i++ instead of i-- in the backwards loop — an infinite loop that freezes the sketch.
  • Collisions that never fire because the character's radius is tiny compared to the art.
  • Removing from the list but forgetting to increase the score, so coins vanish silently.
  • Adding hazards with a backwards loop out of habit. They are not being removed, so a for-each is simpler. Not wrong, just unnecessary.

Wrap-up (~5 min)

Ask: Why do we loop backwards when removing items from a list?

Looking for: Because removing an item shifts everything after it back one position, so a forward loop skips the next item. Going backwards means removals only affect items you already passed.

Teaching notes

  • This is the lesson where it becomes a game rather than a moving picture. Expect energy, and leave time for play-testing.
  • The hazard extension is the most valuable personalization here — it naturally produces the collect/avoid structure Lesson 9 is built on. Push most students toward it.
  • Radius tuning is a real design decision. Frame it that way: "do you want your game generous or precise?"
  • The backwards loop is a copy-and-use pattern, not something to test students on. Do not let it become the hard part of the lesson.
  • Fast finishers: coins that split into smaller blobs on collection, or difficulty that ramps with score.
