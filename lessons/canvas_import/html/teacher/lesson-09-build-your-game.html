Lesson 9

Build Your Game

Everything you have learned, in one game that is yours. This is the one you show people.

TimingTwo periods works best: one to build, one to play-test and present.
New ideaRestarting — and putting all nine lessons together
Builds onEverything. Lessons 1 through 8, all at once.
StandardCombine all prior skills into a complete, playable, replayable mini-game with win/lose conditions and independent design decisions.

Warm-up (~5 min)

Write one sentence describing your game. Who is your character? What are they collecting, and what are they avoiding? Two minutes, before any code.

Direct instruction (~10 min)

Walk the structure — every piece should be familiar

Put the starter on the projector and name each function and where it came from. setup(), draw(), playGame(), showWin(), showGameOver(), restartGame(), mousePressed().

The point of this walkthrough is confidence: they have written all of this before. There is exactly one new function today.

restartGame() — the real lesson here

"To restart, we just run the same setup steps again — create a new player, clear the lists and fill them back up, reset score and gameOver to their starting values."

Point out that setup() is now just a call to restartGame(). Starting and restarting are the same operation, so they are the same code.

This is the most important structural idea in the capstone: reset state by re-running the initialization logic, not by trying to undo individual changes. It is a real software engineering principle and they can absolutely feel why it is better.

Three states and the new operators

playerWon joins gameOver, so draw() now picks between three screens.

&& (AND) and || (OR) show up here for the first time. Introduce them in plain language when you hit them, not as a separate topic: "not lost AND not won means still playing."

Say plainly that the starter is a skeleton

Emphasise this hard: students SHOULD change everything. The blob obstacles are placeholders. Replacing them with something that makes sense in their world is the assignment, not a bonus.

Some students will treat the starter as sacred and only change colors. Head that off in the first five minutes.

Frame the build order

Tell them explicitly: get one full loop working first — player, collectibles, one end condition — and only then build outward.

The students who struggle in this capstone are almost always the ones who tried to add five features before the first one played correctly.

Guided activity (~20 min)

  1. Two minutes of writing: a one-sentence game description. Do this before any code. Students who can say what their game is build much better games.
  2. Run the starter together and identify each piece out loud.
  3. Play until you lose, then click to restart, as a class. Confirm the loop works before anyone changes anything.
  4. Everyone replaces the placeholder collectible with something themed. One quick win, immediately.
  5. Release to build. Circulate constantly. The main intervention you will make all period is "get one thing working before you add the next thing."
  6. Mid-way, stop everyone for five minutes of play-testing with a neighbor. Watching someone else play your game without helping them is the fastest way to find out what is broken.
  7. Final period: demo day. Every student plays a neighbor's game and names one thing they would add.

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Adding features before the core loop works. The number one cause of a frustrated capstone. Intervene early and often.
  • restartGame() that misses a variable — score resets but hazards do not, or the player stays dead. Have them check every global against the reset function, one at a time.
  • Rebuilding ground inside restartGame() is fine, but rebuilding it inside draw() is not. Watch for creation code drifting into the wrong function.
  • Hazards looped backwards unnecessarily — harmless, but a sign they are copying rather than choosing.
  • keyboardControl() coasting: velocity stays set after key release, so the player drifts. If they want it to stop, keyReleased() with setVelocity(0, 0). Fine to introduce if they ask.
  • Difficulty tuned by the person who built it, who is therefore excellent at it. Almost every student game is too hard. Play-testing with a neighbor fixes this.

Wrap-up (~5 min)

Ask: Play a neighbor's game. Tell them one thing you liked and one thing you would add.

Looking for: For the final share-out you want each student to be able to name their game in a sentence and point at one decision they made on purpose. That is the real evidence of the capstone.

Teaching notes

  • The goal is ownership and completion, not polish or correctness. A finished janky game beats an unfinished elegant one, every time.
  • Consider splitting into two periods: period 1 to build, period 2 to play-test and present. The presentation is not optional filler — an audience changes how much students care.
  • Students who finish early should be pushed toward a second mechanic (lives, a power-up, a level 2), not more cosmetics. The extra complexity is where the real learning is.
  • The restartGame() pattern is the structural takeaway. If a student remembers one thing from this lesson in a year, that is the one worth remembering.
  • Bonus Lesson C (Animation) drops in beautifully here — students often want their character to feel alive rather than sliding around as one frozen drawing. Note that an AnimatedSprite is not a Blob, so it uses distanceTo() instead of collidesWith().
  • Bonus Lessons A (Images) and B (Sounds) also land well during this capstone for students who want polish.
  • Watch for students who will not change the starter. Sit with them and ask what their game is *about* — usually they have an idea and did not realize they were allowed to build it.

Rubric reference (no points)

Standard
Combine all prior skills into a complete, playable, replayable mini-game with win/lose conditions and independent design decisions.
Getting StartedThe pieces are not coming together into something playable yet. Next step: get one full loop working first — player, collectibles, and a win or lose check — then build outward. Use restartGame() to reset by re-running your setup steps, not by undoing changes.
Got It WorkingA playable game with my character: collectibles, a win and/or lose condition, a score, and a working restart.
Made It Mine ⭐…and the game is unmistakably mine — themed world, my own rules and difficulty, my character at the center — with design decisions I made on purpose about how it plays.
Went Beyond…and I pushed the game past the assignment in a direction I chose — a second mechanic that genuinely deepens play (lives, an ability, levels, a smart enemy), a rule nobody handed me, something I invented. The mark of this level is a design call that is mine, not cosmetics.

⭐ Made It Mine is the goal for everyone. “Went Beyond” has no fixed list — the examples are only starting points. Going somewhere the lesson did not ask for is the whole idea.
