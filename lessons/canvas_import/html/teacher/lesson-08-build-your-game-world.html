Lesson 8

Build Your Game World

Stop making a sketch and start making a place.

TimingOne period, but it can happily fill two if students are deep in world-building.
New ideaDraw order and layering — and Block for terrain
Builds onLesson 7 — a working collect-and-avoid game.
StandardUse Block for terrain, control draw order for layering, and combine static and moving sprites to design a world.

Warm-up (~5 min)

On paper, sketch the world your game takes place in. What is in the background? Is there ground? Sky? Anything decorating it? Three minutes, then show a neighbor.

Direct instruction (~10 min)

Block, and the corner-versus-center trap

new Block(this, x, y, width, height, color).

x, y is the top-left corner, not the center. This is genuinely confusing after seven lessons of Blob, where it was the center. Say it, write it on the board, and expect to say it again individually.

Block is always stationary — there is no velocity parameter.

A ground strip is the canonical example: new Block(this, 0, height-80, width, 80, color(80, 160, 60)). Walk through why each number is what it is.

Draw order — demonstrate, do not describe

On the projector: draw a big block, then draw a blob on top. Fine. Now reverse the two lines and re-run. The blob vanishes.

That five-second demo teaches the whole concept. Then state the rule: background first, player last.

Reconnect background() to Lesson 1

background() in draw() erases everything before re-drawing. It goes at the top.

The order of calls after background() determines what ends up on top. Students have been doing this since Lesson 5 without a name for it; today it gets the name.

The alpha channel

color(r, g, b, alpha) — alpha 0 is invisible, 255 is fully opaque.

Semi-transparent decorations look genuinely good and cost one number. Show color(255, 255, 255, 180) next to color(255, 255, 255) on the projector.

Push toward sprite classes, not shape piles

Students who want more complex scenery should write a new extends Blob class in a tab, exactly like MyCharacter in Lesson 2 — not pile raw ellipse() and rect() calls into the main tab.

It is the same pattern they already know, it keeps draw() readable, and a decoration built as a sprite can later be given velocity or collision for free.

Guided activity (~20 min)

  1. Start with the paper sketch warm-up. Three minutes of drawing before any code. It genuinely improves what they build.
  2. Everyone adds the ground Block together. Expect the top-left-corner confusion here — several students will put it off-screen.
  3. Everyone changes the background color to something themed. Immediate visible payoff.
  4. Deliberately break it as a class: move ground.drawSprite() to the last line of draw(). Everyone runs and loses their player under the ground. Move it back. Nobody forgets draw order after doing it with their own hands.
  5. Add background decorations together with alpha and a slow drift.
  6. Release for world-building. This is the bulk of the period and it should be.
  7. Last five minutes: quick share-out. Each student shows their world and names one draw-order decision.

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Block x, y as center instead of top-left. The block lands half off-screen. The most common problem in this lesson.
  • Drawing the player before the ground, burying the character.
  • Calling background() somewhere other than the top of draw(), erasing everything drawn that frame.
  • Forgetting .drawSprite() on the ground — the block exists, nothing appears.
  • Piling raw shapes into draw() for decoration instead of writing a sprite class. It works, but draw() becomes unreadable right before the capstone, which is the worst possible time.
  • Decorations drawn with the same speed as gameplay objects, so the depth effect disappears.

Wrap-up (~5 min)

Ask: Name one draw-order decision you made in your world, and what it changed.

Looking for: Anything specific and deliberate: "I drew the clouds before the ground so they look like they are behind the hill," or "I moved the player last so it is never hidden." You want intent, not correctness.

Teaching notes

  • This lesson can easily expand to two periods if students are engaged, and that is a good use of time. The world they build here is the world Lesson 9 happens in, and a student who cares about their world cares more about their game.
  • There is very little new code today. It is a design lesson wearing a programming lesson's clothes. Let it be that.
  • The paper sketch at the start is not filler. Students who plan on paper build noticeably better scenes.
  • Decoration sprite classes written today pay off directly in Lesson 9 — encourage them, especially from fast finishers.
  • Coins and hazards restyled to the theme is the single change that most makes a game feel finished. Push everyone to do at least that.

Rubric reference (no points)

Standard
Use Block for terrain, control draw order for layering, and combine static and moving sprites to design a world.
Getting StartedMy world pieces overlap wrong, or the ground hides my player. Next step: things drawn later appear on top — background first, player last. And Block's x, y is the top-left corner, not the center.
Got It WorkingMy scene has a background, ground or terrain made with Block, and at least one decoration, layered in a sensible order.
Made It Mine ⭐…and I designed a world with a clear theme — themed colors, multiple blocks, restyled coins — that reads as a place, not placeholders.
Went Beyond…and I wrote a new decoration sprite class (Tree, Star, Fish, Rock) in a tab, used transparency and layering, or built platforms or borders that shape how the game plays.

⭐ Made It Mine is the goal for everyone. “Went Beyond” has no fixed list — the examples are only starting points. Going somewhere the lesson did not ask for is the whole idea.
