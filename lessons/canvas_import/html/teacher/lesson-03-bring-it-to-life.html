Lesson 3

Bring It to Life

Your character stops sitting there and starts moving on its own.

TimingOne period (~50 min)
New ideaVelocity — changing position over time
Builds onLesson 2 — your MyCharacter class. Keep that tab exactly as it is.
StandardUse setVelocity() and move() to animate the character, with screen wrapping and random() for variety.

Warm-up (~5 min)

Where does your character get drawn on the screen? Which function draws it, and how many times per second does that happen?

Direct instruction (~10 min)

Draw the axes on the board

Literally draw it. An arrow right labelled +vx, an arrow left labelled -vx, an arrow down labelled +vy, an arrow up labelled -vy.

Positive y being down surprises students every year. Getting it on the board once saves a dozen individual explanations.

Show the failure first

Set a velocity in setup() and run without move(). Nothing happens. Let them sit with that for a few seconds and ask why.

Then add move(). The contrast makes the point better than any explanation: velocity is an intention, move() is the action.

Call order inside draw()

move() first, then drawSprite(). If you draw first and then move, the position is one frame behind.

It is a subtle bug and mostly invisible, but naming it now builds the habit and gives you the wrap-up question.

Let them discover wrapping

Do not announce that sprites wrap. Set a velocity, run it, and let the character slide off the edge while you keep talking.

Someone will shout when it reappears on the other side. That reaction is worth more than telling them.

random() demo

Put random(-5, 5) directly into setVelocity() in setup() and press Run four or five times in a row on the projector. Different every time.

This is also the first time they see a function call used as an argument to another function call. Do not name that. Just let it look normal.

Guided activity (~20 min)

  1. Everyone opens their Lesson 2 sketch. Confirm out loud: the MyCharacter tab does not change today.
  2. Add setVelocity(3, -2) in setup() together. Run. Nothing moves. Ask why before answering.
  3. Add move() in draw(), above drawSprite(). Run. Motion.
  4. Let it run long enough to wrap. Wait for the reaction.
  5. As a class, try fast (8, 0), slow (0.5, 0), and diagonal (3, 3). Ask which direction each one goes before running it.
  6. Swap in random(-5, 5) and have everyone press Run repeatedly.
  7. Release for personalization: tune a speed that fits their character, set a background mood.

Common mistakes to watch for

  • Forgetting move(). By far the most common. The character sits still and students assume setVelocity is broken.
  • Calling drawSprite() before move(). One-frame lag. Usually invisible, but worth a mention.
  • Putting setVelocity() inside draw(). It technically works, but it resets the velocity 60 times a second, which will confuse them badly in Lesson 4 when the keyboard tries to change it.
  • Expecting positive vy to go up. It goes down.
  • Students who drew their Lesson 2 character at hard-coded coordinates will see the hitbox move while the art stays put. This is where that bug surfaces. Fix: re-anchor every shape to pos.x/pos.y.

Wrap-up (~5 min)

Ask: What happens if you call drawSprite() before move()? What does that change?

Looking for: One-frame lag — the character is drawn one step behind where it actually is. Accept any answer that gets at "it draws the old position."

Teaching notes

  • The hard-coded-coordinates bug from Lesson 2 becomes visible today. Expect two or three students to hit it, and treat it as a feature: it is a very clear demonstration of why the belly-button rule exists.
  • Wrapping is a genuine "wow" moment. Protect it by not spoiling it.
  • If a student asks how to make the character bounce off walls instead of wrapping, that is a great question and a great Lesson 9 extension. Park it.
  • Speeds above about 15 make the character hard to watch. Let them discover that rather than capping it.

Rubric reference (no points)

Standard
Use setVelocity() and move() to animate the character, with screen wrapping and random() for variety.
Getting StartedMy character is frozen. Next step: make sure you call player.move() inside draw(). A character with a velocity but no move() never budges.
Got It WorkingMy character moves on its own and wraps around the screen edges.
Made It Mine ⭐…and I chose a velocity that gives my character its own feel — fast, slow, diagonal, or a random() speed — and set a background mood to match.

⭐ Made It Mine is the goal for everyone. “Went Beyond” has no fixed list — the examples are only starting points. Going somewhere the lesson did not ask for is the whole idea.
