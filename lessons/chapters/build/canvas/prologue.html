Prologue · Objects First with Processing

Prologue - How This Book Works

Your character, your world, your rules.

In this book you build a video game:  an actual game, with a character you draw yourself, that you can hand to somebody else and watch them play.

You start on the first day by putting something on the screen.  In Chapter 2 you draw your character.  After that, every chapter gives that character something new.  It moves, then you control it, then it gets a world to live in, then things to collect and things to avoid.  By Chapter 7 the game is yours:  your character, your world, your rules.

You can start this book having never seen a line of code.  It begins at the very beginning.

Objects first

The first thing you make in this book is an object:  a thing in your program that has its own information and knows how to do things.  Plenty of programming courses spend weeks on other things before they get to objects.  This one starts with an object on the first day, and every chapter after that is about objects:  using them, then making your own, then making them do more.

You'll use objects before you know everything inside them.  That's on purpose.  A Blob already knows how to draw itself, how to move, and how to tell when it's touching something.  You'll ask it to do those things, and see them work, a good while before you'd know how to build them yourself.  Curiosity about how it works is the bridge to building your own.

When I was in college, I had a textbook called Objects First with Java.  I didn't realize it at the time, but that book settled my belief that objects are the right place to start, and this book starts with an object for the same reason.

Where this came from

The objects in this book come from a library called SpriteGame.  I wrote it for this class, Coding for Embedded Systems, the sixth-grade class I teach at Winsor.  The class runs for two quarters.  The first quarter is Processing, and that's this book.  The second is Arduino, on the Circuit Playground board.

SpriteGame comes from the same belief about objects first that I teach my high school class with.  The version that went into its repository in 2021 was already about its third, and in the spring of 2026 it became a library you can install into Processing.

The first quarter is Processing because Processing draws.  You write a few lines, press Run, and something shows up on your screen, fast.  That's a good way to start.

How the chapters got written

The chapters came after the lessons.  Each lesson has a plan, a page on Canvas, and the code you start from.  Each chapter got written from those three things.

So the code in every chapter is real.  It's copied from what the lesson gives you, or it's code the chapter asks you to write yourself.  Every picture of a sketch window in this book is a real one:  the code on the page was run, and the picture is what it drew.  When a chapter shows an error message, that's the message Processing printed, word for word.

I wrote this book with a writing assistant:  Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic.  Claude helped me turn each lesson, its code, and its Canvas page into the chapter you're reading.  This book could not exist without that help.

This is the first year the course runs this way, and the book is being written while it runs.  Like the course, it'll change the next time.

How a chapter runs

Most chapters take one class meeting.  Chapter 6 takes two, and Chapter 7 takes three.  All of the building happens in class.

When you get stuck, there's Sparky, an AI helper on Flint.  Sparky helps you with syntax:  the exact spelling, a missing semicolon, what an error message means.  It can help you come up with ideas when you're stuck on what to make.  And it can do the math for you.  If you want a hat sitting right on top of your character's head, Sparky can work out the numbers, and you get back to building.

Your character

From Chapter 2 on, there's one tab in your sketch that belongs entirely to you:  MyCharacter.  Every chapter does its new idea once with an example, so everybody has the same working code and the same words for it.  Then it hands the idea to you, to try on your character.  That's where you find out whether you can do it alone.

Run it after every change

Each chapter's building is cut into small Steps, and your sketch runs at the end of every one.  Run it after every change.  That's how you find out what you broke, while it's still one line.

The chapters come in two units.  Unit 1, Your Character, is Chapters 1 to 4:  you make a character, make it move, and take the controls.  Unit 2, Your Game, is Chapters 5 to 7:  a world, things to collect and avoid, and a game that ends.

How to read a chapter

The shape

Every chapter has the same parts, in the same order.

  • An opening that says where the last chapter left you, and what's still missing.  A table names the chapter's two or three moves.
  • Part One reads the code you start from.  Its sections are numbered by chapter, 1.1, 1.2, 1.3, and each one takes apart one piece of it.
  • Part Two and after build.  The building comes in numbered Steps, and each Step changes one or two things.
  • The end of the chapter gathers it up:  a summary of what you built, a table of new code and where you met it, the vocabulary, a few questions to check your understanding, and a look at what comes next.

The marks

Build it marks code to type into your sketch.  Type it by hand when you can.  Your fingers learn the code faster than your eyes do.

Your turn marks code you write yourself.  The chapter says what it has to do, and leaves the how to you.

Optional marks a Your turn that nothing later depends on.  Skip it if you're short on time, and the steps after it still work.  Do it if you're curious.

The rest come in boxes, and each kind has its own color.

Aside:  what an Aside is

An Aside explains a new piece of code the first time it shows up in the book.  If you already know it, skim the box and keep going.

Check.  A Check marks a place to stop, run your sketch, and make sure it does what the chapter says.  It tells you what you should see, and most Checks come with a picture of the window.  If yours is different, fix that before you go on, because the next step builds on this one.

Watch out.  A Watch out box is the mistake nearly everybody makes at that spot, and what it looks like when you make it.

Argue it.  A question with more than one good answer.  Pick one, and be ready to say why.
